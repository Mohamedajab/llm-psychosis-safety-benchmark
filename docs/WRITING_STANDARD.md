# Writing standard

The future paper should report decisions and evidence, not read like a template filled by a text
generator. [Wikipedia: Signs of AI writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)
is an editing checklist, not a detector or scientific authority.

## Required habits

- Name the dataset, endpoint, estimator, sample size, date, and limitation instead of writing
  “research shows”.
- State what changed, by how much, and with what uncertainty. Adjectives are not results.
- Give each paragraph one job; remove recap sentences that repeat the heading.
- Prefer ordinary verbs such as “is”, “used”, “measured”, and “found”.
- Keep causal language narrower than the design.
- Verify every DOI, author, date, model version, and quoted number against the source.
- Report missing runs, poor agreement, null results, failed assumptions, and deviations.
- Use lists only when items are genuinely parallel; do not force every argument into three points.

## Prohibited shortcuts

- invented citations or placeholder DOIs;
- vague attributions such as “many studies” or “experts believe”;
- claims that the benchmark is pioneering, groundbreaking, comprehensive, or definitive;
- stock endings about a broader landscape or pivotal step;
- decorative “not only X but also Y” constructions;
- repeated endings such as “highlighting the importance of”;
- a generic future-work section unrelated to observed limits;
- hiding methodological choices behind passive voice;
- treating polished prose as evidence that a claim is correct.

For each abstract and conclusion claim, point to a table, figure, protocol decision, or checked
source. If none exists, delete or qualify it. A human author remains responsible for every sentence.
